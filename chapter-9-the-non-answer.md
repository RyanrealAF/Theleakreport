# Chapter 9 — The Non-Answer

---

## Intel Brief

Here's something civilians never account for: silence is not neutral. Silence is not the absence of a signal. Silence, especially right after a direct question, is one of the loudest transmissions you'll ever catch — you just have to stop treating it like empty air and start treating it like a response in its own right.

A non-answer takes a few standard forms, and you need to recognize all of them, because they're not interchangeable. There's **the deflection** — answering a question with a different question, or redirecting focus onto someone else entirely. There's **the topic change** — a hard pivot, sometimes graceful, sometimes clumsy, away from the subject before it can be addressed. There's **the technically-true non-answer** — a real fact that carefully avoids the actual thing being asked. And there's **the flat dodge** — a joke, a "why do you ask," a laugh that buys time without giving anything at all.

What all four have in common: a direct question creates a fork in the road, and a real answer is only one of the paths available. Every non-answer is a choice to take a different path — and that choice itself is data, independent of whatever words eventually came out.

Here's the doctrine line to remember: **a non-answer is not a failure to respond. It's a decision about what not to reveal, executed in real time.** Somebody had to actively steer around the direct answer. That steering took effort, even if it looked effortless. And effort spent avoiding something tells you the something was worth avoiding.

**Academic ground:** Communication researchers studying "equivocation" — the technical term for intentionally vague or evasive responses — have found people equivocate most heavily precisely in situations where every available direct answer carries some kind of cost, whether that's damaging a relationship, admitting fault, or revealing something they're not ready to reveal (Bavelas, Black, Chovil, & Mullett, 1990, on equivocal communication theory). The vague answer isn't a failure of communication. It's often the most strategically calculated thing a person says in the whole exchange.

**Silence after a direct question isn't nothing. It's the answer wearing a disguise.**

## Street Recon

**Scene 1 — The Family Phone Call**
A mother asks her adult son straight out, "Are you and your wife doing okay? You seem off lately." He responds, "Work's been crazy, honestly, I barely have time to think about anything else these days." She didn't ask about work. He answered a question she didn't ask, at length, with real detail — enough detail to feel like a full answer without ever touching the one she actually put on the table. *A detailed answer to the wrong question is still a non-answer, just a well-disguised one.*

**Scene 2 — The Group Chat**
Someone asks directly, in a group chat planning a birthday dinner, "Hey are you still not talking to Jordan? Should we not invite him?" The person it's aimed at responds with a laughing emoji and "lol let's just figure out the restaurant first." Nobody follows up. The plan moves forward without the question ever getting answered. *The joke and the pivot did the same job — steer the conversation away before an answer had to exist.*

**Scene 3 — The Workplace**
A manager asks an employee point blank, "Are you looking for other jobs right now?" The employee says, "I mean, I'm always open to opportunities, who isn't, right?" Technically true. Answers nothing. The manager nods and moves the meeting along. *A statement that's true for literally everyone on earth is specifically designed to feel like an answer while functioning as a wall.*

Three completely different question types — emotional, social, professional — and three variations on the same core move: redirect, deflect, or answer something technically true but functionally empty. None of these people lied. None of these people answered, either.

## Action Steps

1. **Name which type of non-answer you just heard.**
   Deflection, topic change, technically-true dodge, or flat joke-and-move-on — each one tells you something slightly different about how much control the person felt like they had in that moment. A calm redirect and a panicked joke are not the same signal.

2. **Track what the non-answer replaced.**
   Go back to your original question and hold it next to what actually came back. The gap between the two — what got asked versus what got answered — is the shape of the thing being avoided, even if you never learn what it specifically is.

3. **Resist the urge to re-ask immediately.**
   Pushing a second time in the same conversation usually just triggers a more polished non-answer, not a real one — now they've had time to build a better wall. Let it sit. The information you already have — that they steered away — may be all you get today, and that's still real intel.

4. **Weigh the cost of a real answer from their seat, not yours.**
   Before deciding a non-answer means something damning, consider what a direct answer would have actually cost them in that moment — embarrassment, conflict, vulnerability. Sometimes the avoidance is about the discomfort of the question itself, not a hidden crime underneath it. This is the Ch. 5 code in practice: don't convict someone of guilt when what you caught might just be someone protecting their own dignity.

5. **Log the topic that got swerved, not just the swerve.**
   Note exactly what subject caused the redirect. Over time, a running list of "topics this person always steers away from" builds a far more useful profile than any single non-answer ever could on its own.

## Quick Debrief

Silence after a direct question isn't empty. It's the answer in disguise.
The gap between what you asked and what came back is the shape of what's hidden.
Don't push twice in the same breath — you'll just get a better wall.
A non-answer isn't a failure to speak. It's a decision, made in real time.
