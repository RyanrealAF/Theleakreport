# Chapter 10 — Overcorrection

---

## Intel Brief

A denial should cost about as much energy as the accusation did. Somebody says "did you eat the last slice," you say "no," done, transmission complete, nobody's overdrawn on effort. That's calibration. Overcorrection is what happens when the response spends way more energy than the moment required — more words, more emphasis, more repetition than the situation could possibly justify — and that excess is the leak.

Chapter 1 already showed you the base case: three denials in one breath, "I've got no problem, I'm not — it's not a problem for me." That's the flagship pattern of this whole chapter, so let's name the mechanism properly. Overcorrection happens because the internal editor isn't just managing what gets said — it's managing how *convincing* it needs to sound, and it's miscalibrating both at once. A person who's actually fine doesn't need to sell you on being fine. Selling implies there's a doubt in the room that needs closing, and usually, the doubt started with them, not you.

There are two flavors worth separating. **Over-explaining** — piling on detail, justification, and context nobody asked for, as if a bigger pile of words will bury the discomfort underneath it. **Over-denying** — repeating the same denial multiple times, in slightly different phrasing, like the first version didn't convince even the person saying it. Both run on the same fuel: something underneath needed managing, and the management effort leaked out sideways as excess.

**Academic ground:** Psychological research on thought suppression has repeatedly found what's sometimes called the "ironic process" — actively trying not to think about or reveal something tends to make that exact thing more present and more likely to surface, not less (Wegner, 1994, on ironic process theory, sometimes summarized as the "white bear" effect). The harder the suppression effort, the more the suppressed content bleeds through the cracks around it. Overcorrection is that bleed, happening out loud, in real time, in front of you.

**The size of the denial tells you the size of what's underneath it. A small no doesn't need three tries.**

## Street Recon

**Scene 1 — The Anniversary Dinner**
She asks him, half-joking, if he's been texting his coworker a lot lately. He says, "No, I mean, we just work on the same project, it's purely professional, there's genuinely nothing there, I promise you it's completely fine." Four separate assurances stacked on one casual question. She laughs it off in the moment and changes the subject. *A question that small didn't need an answer that big — the size of the denial is the actual tell, not the content of it.*

**Scene 2 — The Team Standup**
A coworker asks another, lightly, "Did you finish the report last night or are we still waiting on it?" The guy responds, "Yeah, no, I mean, I was going to, I got caught up with some stuff, but it's basically done, I just need to format it, it'll be ready, I promise, don't worry about it." One casual check-in triggered a full paragraph of justification for something that hadn't even been criticized yet. *He was defending against an accusation nobody made — which means he'd already made it against himself.*

**Scene 3 — The Family Text Thread**
A sibling asks in the family group chat, "Are you coming to Mom's birthday this year?" The response: "Yes!! Of course, wouldn't miss it, I already requested the day off work, I've had it marked on my calendar for months, I'm so excited, tell her I said hi." A yes/no question got a five-sentence victory lap. *Nobody doubted the answer until the answer showed up working this hard to be believed.*

None of these three were caught in an actual lie — that's the important part. Overcorrection often shows up around things that are technically true, or true-ish, but carry some discomfort the person is trying to manage in advance. The overcorrection is the management showing through the seams.

## Action Steps

1. **Measure the response against the size of the question.**
   A small, casual question should get a proportionally small answer. When the response arrives dramatically oversized — extra detail, extra repetition, extra emphasis — that size mismatch is the actual data point, regardless of what the words say.

2. **Count the repeats.**
   One denial is just an answer. Two or three variations of the same denial in one breath is the pattern this chapter is named for. Note how many times they said essentially the same thing in different words — that number matters more than any single phrase.

3. **Separate over-explaining from over-denying, because they point differently.**
   Over-explaining usually means guilt or discomfort about something adjacent to the topic. Over-denying usually means the direct question landed closer to a real nerve than the person wants to admit. Don't treat them as identical signals.

4. **Don't escalate just because you noticed the mismatch.**
   The instinct is to push harder the second you clock overcorrection — "why are you being so defensive." That instinct usually backfires, producing more overcorrection, not less. Sit with what you caught. This is the Ch. 5 code doing its job: understanding where the pressure point is, not detonating it in the moment for a reaction.

5. **Watch what happens the next time the same topic comes up.**
   A one-time overcorrection might just be an off moment. If the same subject reliably produces oversized responses every time it surfaces, that's no longer a blip — that's a mapped pressure point, and it belongs in your long-term pattern tracking from Chapter 16.

## Quick Debrief

A small question doesn't need three denials to answer it.
Over-explaining and over-denying are cousins, but they point to different nerves.
Count the repeats. Don't push the second you notice them.
The size of the "no" tells you the size of what's actually underneath it.
